Silver just crossed $100 an ounce — and fear had almost nothing to do with it.

If you own silver jewellery, a silver ETF, or you're quoting a rooftop solar install, you've already felt this move. For a century silver was just the metal you bought when you couldn't afford gold. Now the sun buys more of it than the vault does — and that quiet switch is rewriting the rules of a 5,000-year-old market.

Ask most people what silver is for, and they'll say a coin, a candlestick, or a cheaper way into gold. That was true for most of the twentieth century. It isn't true anymore — the market has been quietly telling us so for about a decade.

Here's the mechanism, in three sentences. A solar cell needs a conductive paste to carry electricity off the panel, and silver conducts better than any other element there is. So every new panel needs a little silver. The world is installing solar panels faster than at any point in history — which means it needs more silver, faster than at any point in history, too.

In 2024 that added up to 680.5 million ounces of silver used in industry — a record — and nearly a third of it went into that one paste, on the back of a solar cell.1 Silver isn't riding on gold's coattails anymore. It's become load-bearing infrastructure for the energy transition — and that's already changing how the metal behaves, who fights over it, and what its price means for you.

01 — TWO IDENTITIES, ONE METAL

The metal has always had a split personality. Only now is one half winning.

Silver has spent its whole monetary life underneath gold, both literally and in the popular imagination. It's rarer to mine on its own — roughly three-quarters of the world's silver arrives as a byproduct of digging for lead, zinc, copper, and gold, not because anyone went looking for it.2 That byproduct status is exactly why the "poor man's gold" label stuck: cheaper, more accessible, priced as an emotional echo of the metal above it.

But underneath that reputation, silver has always led a second life — the best electrical and thermal conductor of any element on the periodic table, more reflective of light than any other metal, resistant to the corrosion that ruins cheaper conductors.3 For most of the 20th century that second identity was a footnote: photography, mirrors, tableware. Now it's the headline.

THE OLD STORY — MONETARY METAL

Priced against gold. Bought in coins and bars as a cheaper way into the precious metals trade. Demand rises and falls with fear, inflation expectations, and what the gold price is doing. Industrial use is a rounding error at the bottom of the press release.

THE NEW STORY — INDUSTRIAL INPUT

Priced against what it costs to build a solar cell, an EV sensor, or an AI server's connector. Demand rises with global electrification. Supply is inelastic because nobody mines silver for silver. Investors now watch solar installation data the way they used to watch inflation prints.

Both identities are still real. What's new is the balance of power between them — and 2025–26 was the year the industrial identity started setting the terms.

02 — THE TAKEOVER, IN ONE CHART

Solar's share of silver's industrial demand, 2014 → 2026

In 2014, photovoltaics accounted for roughly 11% of the silver used in industry — a modest, second-tier application behind electronics, brazing alloys, and ethylene oxide catalysis.4 By 2024, that share had nearly tripled to 29%.1 Look at the raw ounces and the story gets starker still.

2024's spike reflects faster adoption of new-generation, more silver-intensive TOPCon cells replacing older PERC technology — before manufacturers had scaled thrifting in response.5 The 2025–26 dip is thrifting finally catching up with cost pressure, not solar installations slowing down. Global PV capacity kept growing throughout.

The shape of that curve is the whole story compressed into one line. A gentle climb through the 2010s. Then a nearly vertical rise in 2022–2024, when demand jumped 64% in a single year (2023) as manufacturers rushed new higher-silver cell architectures into production faster than anyone expected.6 Then, just as suddenly, a sharp reversal in 2025 and 2026 — not because solar stopped growing, but because the industry finally started fighting back against its own silver bill.

03 — THE DECOUPLING

Ten times more solar. Three times more silver. That gap is the whole plot.

Here's the number that should reframe how you read every other statistic in this piece: global installed photovoltaic capacity increased more than tenfold over the decade through 2024. Silver demand from photovoltaics, over the same period, increased only threefold.1

That gap didn't happen by accident. It's the cumulative result of "thrifting" — a slow, relentless engineering campaign to shave fractions of a gram of silver paste off every single solar cell, year after year, because silver is the single most expensive material in a panel's bill of materials after the silicon wafer itself.

Think of it like a recipe quietly rewritten to use less of an expensive spice, not a different spice altogether. That's thrifting: thinner busbars, finer screen-printing, better pastes, all doing the same job with less silver. Substitution is the more radical move — replacing silver with copper entirely. In 2026 the Silver Institute was explicit that the industry is still overwhelmingly doing the first thing, not the second: manufacturers are using less, not yet using something else.7 That distinction matters enormously for what happens next.

The decoupling index

Indexed to 100 at 2014 levels. PV capacity outgrew silver demand roughly 3.3-to-1 over the decade — the visible fingerprint of thrifting at industrial scale.

04 — THE PARADOX

Solar used 19% less silver in 2026. The shortage got worse anyway.

This is the counterintuitive heart of the story, and it's the part most headlines miss. If thrifting worked — if engineers really did solve the silver problem — the market should have loosened. It didn't.

Solar's silver consumption fell from 186.6 million ounces in 2025 to roughly 151 million ounces in 2026: a 19% cut, the largest single-year reduction on record for the sector.8 Meanwhile the global silver market recorded its sixth consecutive annual deficit — and that deficit widened, from 40.3 million ounces in 2025 to a forecast 46.3 million ounces in 2026.9

The resolution to the paradox sits on the supply side, not the demand side. Roughly three out of every four ounces of silver mined comes up as a byproduct of digging for lead, zinc, copper, or gold.2 Mining companies don't open a new pit because silver prices spiked — they open it because zinc or copper prices justify it, and silver just comes along for the ride. So silver supply is structurally sluggish, almost deaf to its own price signal. When elevated prices finally did coax more supply out of the ground in 2025 — mine output rose 3% and recycling hit a 13-year high of 197.6 million ounces — it narrowed the deficit slightly, but didn't close it, and analysts don't expect the same response to repeat in 2026.9,10

"The deficit is widening because supply is contracting faster than demand — both sides of the ledger are shrinking, and the gap is growing anyway."

06 — THE COMPETITION FOR EVERY OUNCE

Solar isn't the only new claimant at the table anymore

While solar was busy thrifting itself down, other industrial buyers were quietly stepping into the silver market for the first time at scale: AI data-center hardware, EV electronics, and grid infrastructure. None of them individually rival solar's appetite, but together they're changing the shape of demand.12

07 — WHAT THE PRICE HAS BEEN SAYING

Silver stopped trading like gold's shadow around 2024

The gold-silver ratio — how many ounces of silver it takes to buy one ounce of gold — is the market's oldest gauge of whether silver is "cheap" relative to its senior partner. For fifty years it has swung mostly between 40 and 80, with a long-run average near 60.14 In April 2025 it spiked past 100, a historic extreme that signalled silver was deeply undervalued even by its own volatile standards.15

What happened next is the tell. Silver didn't just catch up to gold — it tore through $100 an ounce for the first time in January 2026, compressing the ratio below 50, a level not seen since 2012.16 Put another way: an ounce of silver that cost about $12 at the depths of the March 2020 crash now costs more than eight times that, for the same ounce. A move that dramatic, that fast, is no longer explainable purely as monetary flight-to-safety. It's what happens when a genuine physical deficit collides with a wave of investment demand at the same time.

08 — THE NEXT MOVE: FROM THRIFTING TO SUBSTITUTION

Thrifting has limits. The industry's real endgame is copper.

Academic modelling backs up what the market data is already showing: silver's binding constraint in solar isn't environmental — it's cost and supply security.18 A 2026 sustainability framework for crystalline-silicon photovoltaics published in Solar RRL found that further thrifting, alternative pastes, and — eventually — copper-based metallization are the main levers left to keep silver from becoming a hard ceiling on how fast solar can scale.18

The IEA's PVPS Task 12 modelling of material flows through mid-century reaches a similar conclusion from a different angle: substituting silver with copper-based metallization could substantially cut the sector's silver demand, while some proposed alternative cell technologies — those leaning on high shares of indium, for instance — would trade one resource constraint for another.19 There is no free lunch here, only different bottlenecks.

A separate forecasting study published in Resources, Conservation and Recycling modelled the 2030 horizon directly and arrived at a genuinely uncomfortable number: even under a range of realistic PV deployment and thrifting scenarios, supply is projected to cover only 62–70% of total silver demand of 48,000–54,000 tonnes a year — meaning the structural deficit doesn't close on its own. It persists, or it widens.20

Two paths forward for PV silver demand to 2030

Illustrative range built from published 2030 forecasts: a "thrifting continues" path holds PV's share near 30–34% of industrial demand; a "substitution accelerates" path — copper largely replacing silver metallization — could pull that share down meaningfully faster.19,20

Why hasn't copper already won?

Copper is roughly a hundred times cheaper than silver by weight, conducts electricity almost as well, and there's no shortage of it. The obstacle isn't chemistry, it's manufacturing. Silver paste is compatible with existing screen-printing production lines the entire solar industry has spent two decades optimizing. Copper metallization needs different plating processes, tighter contamination control, and new capital equipment — the kind of retooling a margin-thin, hyper-competitive industry does reluctantly and slowly, even once the underlying economics start to favor it.18,19 That lag is exactly why thrifting — the cheap, incremental fix — has done almost all the work so far, and why substitution remains more a live experiment than a market fact today.

09 — WHAT THIS ACTUALLY CHANGES

Silver now has two masters, and they don't always agree

The practical consequence of everything above is that silver's price no longer answers to a single logic. It used to move mostly on fear, inflation expectations, and whatever gold was doing. Now it also has to answer to the balance sheet of every solar module manufacturer on Earth, the ramp schedule of AI data centers, and the pace at which lead and zinc miners decide to expand — because that's where most of its supply is quietly coming from, as a byproduct.2

That's a structurally different asset than the one implied by "poor man's gold." A monetary metal that briefly gets more expensive because people are scared is a familiar, cyclical story. A metal that's structurally short because it sits inside a technology the entire world is deploying at record pace — one where every year of demand-side engineering to use less of it still hasn't closed the gap — is a different kind of story, closer to how markets talk about lithium or cobalt than how they've traditionally talked about silver.

None of this means silver has fully shed its old identity. Coin and bar demand actually rose sharply through 2025 and into 2026, pulled higher by exactly the kind of macro anxiety silver has always responded to — stagflation worries, central bank independence questions, a search for safe-haven assets.21 The old story and the new one are now running at the same time, sometimes reinforcing each other, sometimes pulling in opposite directions. That tension — not a clean handoff from "monetary" to "industrial" — is the real shape of silver's transformation.

The next time someone calls it "poor man's gold," ask them what's on the back of their solar panel.

Silver's old identity isn't dead — people will keep buying coins in a panic for as long as there are panics to buy them in. But the metal's center of gravity has shifted somewhere the coin buyers rarely look: onto the roof, into the inverter, and increasingly, given the trajectories in front of it, into the copper alternative the industry is racing to perfect before the deficit forces its hand.

What happens to silver next depends less on what central banks do and more on how fast solar manufacturers can finish an engineering project that's already been running for a decade — and on how much of the world's lead, zinc, and copper mining expands in the meantime, since that's still where most new silver will come from, whether anyone was looking for it or not.
